# Sashiko & Visible Mending

**Tier:** physical-output · **Rubric:** Pop 2 · Pain 2 · Fit 5 · GapCost 5 · Adopters 2 = **200** · Longlist B5

## Snapshot
Sashiko's repeating stitch geometries (hitomezashi, moyōzashi families) are
parametric pattern math having a genuine fashion moment — Gen Z visible
mending is in Etsy's 2026 seller report, and the craft's grids are buildable
in Pathogen today.

## Description
Stitchers marking repeating geometric grids on fabric (traditionally indigo)
and stitching running-stitch patterns; visible menders applying the same
vocabulary to worn denim. Patterns are transferred via printed templates,
stencils, or pre-printed cloth. The pattern families are mathematically
crisp: hitomezashi emerges from row/column binary sequences; moyōzashi are
interlocking curve grids.

## Problems Pathogen could address
Pattern generation is the product: templates at exact stitch pitch, scaled
to a garment patch, tiled seamlessly, exported as print-transfer sheets or
stencil cut files (bridging to A3 stencils). Hitomezashi's sequence-driven
emergence is a delightful computational story (a known math-art crossover).
Low pain — the craft values hand process — but near-zero feature gap.

## Commercial value
Template/stencil sellers, workshop teachers (an active 2026 class economy),
kit makers. Small; the strategic value is content — the math-art crossover
writes itself.

## Missing features
### Domain-specific [D]
- Stitch-pitch-aware dashed rendering (stitches as dashes at true pitch)
- Patch-shaped tiling (fill an arbitrary mend outline with pattern)
- Transfer-sheet output conventions (mirror for iron-on)
### General [G]
- Physical units (stitch pitch in mm); almost nothing else — the purest
  low-gap domain on the list

## Project ideas (as of 2026-10-07)
Tiered by Pathogen skill and readiness. Web-researched; a dated snapshot.

### Beginner — buildable today
- **Hitomezashi name doodle** — Hitomezashi reached a wide audience as a
  pencil-and-graph-paper drawing: write a phrase along each edge of a grid,
  start each row's dashes on or off according to whether its letter is a
  vowel, and a maze-like pattern appears. A Grid and dashed lines reproduce
  it, with the two on-off sequences typed in as arrays. It is finished as a
  screen image or a printed card at any size.
- **Pattern family reference sheet** — Suppliers and teachers hand out sheets
  showing the traditional patterns side by side with their names: asanoha,
  seigaiha, shippō, sayagata, kagome. Each swatch is a small tile of one
  pattern, clipped to a square and labelled, and Grid lays the swatches out.
  It is a recognition chart, not a stitching template, so it prints at
  whatever size the page allows and no stitch length is implied.

### Intermediate — several features, or one gap
- **Hitomezashi coaster template** — A beginner coaster features in many
  sashiko starter kits: a small square of grid cloth stitched in rows, then
  columns, until a woven-looking pattern appears. Two short on-off sequences
  decide where each row and column starts, and a grid with dashed lines draws
  it. Reproducing the kit's pre-printed cloth depends on physical units, so
  the grid has a stated 5 mm pitch; until then the maker scales the drawing by
  hand.
- **Asanoha sampler panel** — Asanoha, the hemp-leaf star, is among the first
  moyōzashi patterns teachers assign and a common choice for tote-bag panels.
  It is straight lines on a triangular grid, drawn as dashes and tiled across
  a panel, with each line direction as its own pass to show stitching order.
  The known dependency is physical units: panel size and stitch length are
  millimetre values, converted by hand until they can be written directly.
- **Shippō-tsunagi hanafukin** — A hanafukin is the traditional stitched
  kitchen cloth; shippō-tsunagi, or seven treasures, covers it in interlocking
  circles. The pattern is one circle tiled on a square grid at half-diameter
  spacing and dashed, a first step from straight pattern lines to curved ones.
  A tracing template for a cloth of fixed size depends on physical units;
  until then circle diameter and stitch length are worked out by hand before
  printing.
- **Pounce-stencil pattern sheet** — Many stitchers transfer patterns with a
  plastic stencil and chalk or a marking pen. The stencil version of a pattern
  replaces continuous lines with slots separated by bridges so the sheet holds
  together. Deriving both the print template and the bridged cut file from one
  pattern is the pattern-to-stencil bridge the profile proposes, using dashes
  and tiling together.

### Advanced — depends on a named gap
- **Pattern-filled jacket back panel** — The showpiece of the current sashiko
  revival is a chore coat or denim jacket with the whole back yoke covered in
  pattern, as in the fashion collaborations. The pattern must stop cleanly at
  an irregular outline. That depends on patch-shaped tiling, filling an
  arbitrary outline with pattern, which is listed as missing and matters for
  every real garment.
- **Iron-on transfer sheet pack** — Kit makers and teachers sell patterns as
  iron-on transfer sheets or printed wash-away stabiliser. Iron-on transfers
  must be mirrored, with margins, repeat marks and a scale check the buyer can
  trust. It depends on the transfer-sheet output conventions listed as missing
  — a small feature that would let a template seller ship directly from a
  program.
- **Curved moyōzashi with correct crossings** — In traditional practice
  stitches do not cross at pattern intersections; they meet with a small gap,
  and stitch counts between crossings stay even. On curved patterns such as
  fundō or linked circles this takes careful planning. It depends on
  stitch-pitch-aware dashed rendering — stitches as dashes at true pitch,
  placed relative to intersections, not simply by distance.
